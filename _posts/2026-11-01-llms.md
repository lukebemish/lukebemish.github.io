Recently I've been trying to put my thoughts in order about LLMs, and generative "AI" more broadly. The short version is: I don't like them, and I feel like most uses I see of them are not particularly innovative or interesting, and I think we stand to lose a lot thanks to peoples' increasing dependence on them. But there's a bit more nuance to how I think about LLMs than that, so I'll try to put at least some of that into words.

> [!NOTE]
> A note on terminology: I'm going to try and avoid the term "AI" if at all possible because it's unfortunately used to refer to an absolutely enormous plethora of entirely unrelated things. Of course, when people talk about "AI" now they mostly mean large language models or wrappers around them, the sort that _certain companies_ would have us believe are "artificial general intelligence" of some sort. Unfortunately, the term has also been used historically to refer to models of a completely different sort: e.g., automatic image segmentation algorithms or the like. Since there's something rather qualitatively different about the former compared to the latter, I try to just refer to the latter with specific terms as needed, and the former either as LLMs or (if I'm talking more generally) as artificial pseudointelligence, and leave the term "AI" out of the picture.

My first run-in with large language models (that I can think of) was trying out the technical preview of GitHub Copilot, which at the time was not terribly powerful. You could ask it to write some code, it would struggle terribly and you'd get out some absolute nonsense that halfway did what you wanted. You could, instead, have it complete the rest of a line that you'd started writing, and it would -- especially in cases where there is only one sensible way to complete that line, from the surrounding context -- do a passable job at finishing the line for you. The cost, of course, being the latency of waiting for it to make a round trip to GitHub's servers, in addition to whatever time it took to process on that end. This seemed neat, but not terribly useful, so I mostly forgot about it. As an aside: IntelliJ IDEs now have a feature called "Full Line Code Completion" for basically exactly this, completing single lines of code, but using a local model instead[^1]. This is one of very few uses of this technology that I've found to be at all useful; it's a logical complement for IntelliSense or other IDE autocomplete tools.

The next time I ran into LLMs was likely during fall 2023; at this point ChatGPT had started to take off, and I (in undergrad at the time) began to see classmates trying to use LLMs to write their essays for them. By and large, this did not work out too well, but it was the start of a larger trend normalizing the involvment of LLMs in everyday tasks like writing.
