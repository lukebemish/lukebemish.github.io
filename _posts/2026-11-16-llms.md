Recently I've been trying to put my thoughts in order about LLMs, and generative "AI" more broadly. The short version is: I don't like it, and I feel like most uses I see of it are not particularly innovative or interesting, and I think we stand to lose a lot thanks to peoples' increasing dependence on it. But there's a bit more nuance to how I think about LLMs than that, so I'll try to put at least some of that into words.

> [!NOTE]
> A note on terminology: I'm going to try and avoid the term "AI" if at all possible because it's unfortunately used to refer to an absolutely enormous plethora of entirely unrelated things. Of course, when people talk about "AI" now they mostly mean large language models or wrappers around them, the sort that _certain companies_ would have us believe are "artificial general intelligence" of some sort. Unfortunately, the term has also been used historically to refer to models of a completely different sort: e.g., automatic image segmentation algorithms or the like. Since there's something rather qualitatively different about the former compared to the latter, I try to just refer to the latter with specific terms as needed, and the former either as LLMs or (if I'm talking more generally) as artificial pseudointelligence, and leave the term "AI" out of the picture.

My first run-in with large language models (that I can think of) was trying out the technical preview of GitHub Copilot, which at the time was not terribly powerful. You could ask it to write some code, it would struggle terribly and you'd get out some absolute nonsense that halfway did what you wanted. You could, instead, have it complete the rest of a line that you'd started writing, and it would -- especially in cases where there is only one sensible way to complete that line, from the surrounding context -- do a passable job at finishing the line for you.

The cost for this, of course, was the latency of waiting for it to make a round trip to GitHub's servers, in addition to whatever time it took to process on that end. This seemed neat, but not terribly useful, so I mostly forgot about it. As an aside: IntelliJ IDEs now have a feature called "<a href="https://blog.jetbrains.com/blog/2024/04/04/full-line-code-completion-in-jetbrains-ides-all-you-need-to-know/" rel="nofollow noreferrer">Full Line Code Completion</a>" for basically exactly this, completing single lines of code, but using a local model instead. This is one of very few uses of this technology that I've found to be at all useful; it's a logical complement for IntelliSense or other IDE autocomplete tools, and does basically nothing except in cases where there's really only one logical way to finish a line, in which case it lets you type the same stuff faster.[^1]

[^1]: It's also, I will note, something you can run locally on a machine, doesn't even require all that powerful of a computer to do so, and doesn't stand to gain much by throwing more power or a fancier model at it. All that to say that even for this use case, I don't see a niche for the sort of offerings OpenAI/Anthropic/etc. are selling.

The next time I ran into LLMs was during fall 2023; at this point ChatGPT had started to take off, and I (in undergrad at the time) began to see classmates trying to use LLMs to write their essays for them. By and large, this did not work out too well, but it was the start of a larger trend normalizing the involvement of LLMs in everyday tasks like writing.

It was at this point that LLMs, which I had not really interacted with much before then, began to leave a bad taste in my mouth. The increasing prevalance of LLMs in academic contexts, or perhaps more importantly, the way that my classmates talked about them, seems to be indicative of a more broad misunderstanding about education: LLMs appealed to the lazy student as a way to cheat, but they appealed to the otherwise quite hardworking student as a way to be _more efficient_. After all, why not use an LLM —[^3] if not to write, than at least to brainstorm, "work through" a math problem, or the like — if it helps you finish your course work more quickly? Especially if, as the claim went, use of LLMs to brainstorm or to help study didn't impede your understanding of the material, since you were the one still doing the actual work.

[^3]: Yes, I occasionally use em-dashes... I would like to think my writing abuses parentheticals less than the average LLM-produced slop, but. Em-dashes are useful sometimes.

I've seen arguments like this pop up more and more since then, as the technology has gotten "better" at (or, well, has gotten better at sounding like its good at) helping people study, put their thoughts in order, summarize a document, perform a literature search, or the like. And I think the arguments all sort of miss the point in terms of why we do most of the tasks in question.

Reading Shakespeare's words will always be a different experience than reading the actual text of, I don't know, _Othello_. When we read _Othello_, we're not doing so for the information we get from a summary of the plot. We're interested in details that come from Shakespeare's artistry with language as much as anything. Because these details are aesthetic in nature, we can only understand them by reading the play; it's impossible to understand the beauty of the writing without actually reading it. Because these details are not _purely_ aesthetic (Shakespeare communicates meaning with his language, after all), you lose out on your understanding of the work without seeing them. I don't think this is particularly controversial; the summary must be shorter than the whole work, and plenty of authors are quite concise with their language.

But! The doubter shouts, An LLM can do so much more than summarize! You can talk to it!. I will note, at this point, that you could also talk with a human, and it would likely be far more valuable. LLMs — even if we were to talk about them in a way that assigns personhood, which I despise — are generic. "They" (the text they generate) certainly contain all sorts of wacky biases and quirks of prose, but the output they produce is going to look much more homogenous than what humans do. This is a necessary outcome of LLMs not being people, and thus not going into a conversation with a distinct outlook on like predicated by their experiences.[^2] Talking with a friend about a text will steer your thinking on the text in all sorts of wild directions, but they'll be different directions for different people, and they'll be directions informed by the other person's experiences and beliefs, not pulled from some melted-together vat.

[^2]: There is a school of thought that would claim that LLMs _do_ have personhood. I'll not comment here on whether it's possible to make a machine with personhood, since I think it's beside the point, but I will say that modern LLMs definitely don't have anything approaching it: producing text that sounds like a person wrote it is not what constitutes personhood. Being a _person_, with connections to people in the way that people do, and the worries that people have, is. Yes, I know that's a bit circular, but this is just an aside anyhow, and if we _did_ eventually create a machine with personhood then using it for any of the things mentioned in this article — and any of what people currently use LLMs for — would be wildly unethical, so...

## LLMs in Science

I used the example of Shakespeare earlier, but I'm a biologist, not a philologist. And (unfortunately) that means that since undergrad I've not really engaged with (non-scientific) literature in an academic setting. What I have done, however, is read papers; lots and lots of papers. I've also discovered that perceptions on "valid" uses of LLMs are... a bit more permissive in the STEM world than they were at the liberal arts college I attended for undergrad.

Should we use LLMs to help search through scientific literature? Should we use LLMs to write code for data analyses? Should we use LLMs to help edit an email, or even a paper, to make the language more clean, or more clear, or more concise? I would argue "no" on all counts.

The point of reading papers and searching the scientific literature is not merely to come up with the answer to a specific question, but to establish a mental model for research in a field, the different avenues people are exploring, and the blind spots — whether they be due to technical limitations or just lack of research. Science, like any research, is a collaborative process; all work we do is built off the work others did, and so doing science necessarily implies being familiar with the work others are doing!

Using an LLM to assist in a literature search distances you from the actual text; how can you expect to build a familiarity with the field, or to extend your existing familiarity, when you are letting a machine guide which texts you read, or summarize them outright?

Using an LLM to assist in looking through literature is either the sort of searching where the amount of summarizing and otherwise distorting that the LLM does risks impeding your ability to actually understand the text, or it's the sort of search you could have done without an LLM before just fine. I think in many instances the LLM is nothing more than a paint job, showing us exactly the same things we'd have found by searching the normal way, surrounded by human language that fools us into thinking there's something more special about it.

I'll come back to writing code, since I think it fits better in a later section (for reasons that will I hope become apparent).

Writing with an LLM is troubling, I think, for several reasons. I think there is a rather clear-cut argument that LLM-written text should never appear in published works:

- It's dishonest to represent text as your own that was not written by you
- There is an implicit assumption when someone is reading your text that what they are reading is _your_ thoughts; giving them something else disrespectful of their time.

And i think the same argument applies to any case of writing something that someone else is going to read (e.g., and email). I also think this is, in the biological sciences at least, not yet controversial, whatever horror stories I may hear from friends in more physics adjacent spaces.
